# US Economics Analyst: Productivity Stagnation in the Construction Industry: An International Perspective

- Between 1970 and 2024, aggregate labor productivity in the US more than doubled. In stark contrast, labor productivity in the construction industry declined by 30% over the same period. The persistent decline in construction productivity appears to be a common trend across advanced economies but it has been most acute in the US, contributing to its housing shortage and affordability crisis. We explore why the US construction industry’s productivity has underperformed relative to both the rest of the US economy and other countries.
- One partial explanation is that innovation related to construction has been limited. Most of the major machines used for construction today had already been invented in the 1950s. Since then, technological changes within construction have been incremental, and the industry has also benefitted relatively little from rapid advances in information and communication. Using industry-level patent data, we estimate that technological changes have boosted annual construction productivity growth by 0.8pp since 1965, below the 1-1.3pp boost that we estimate for other industries.
- Increased regulation of housing has also been a major drag on construction productivity growth. New data reveal that land use regulations have gradually become more stringent across major advanced economies over the past 50 years, with regulations tightening the most in the US. Our cross-country analysis shows that regulatory changes lowered annual construction productivity growth by 0.7pp, offsetting much of the boost from improvements in technology and labor quality. For more recent decades, in which more data are available for other advanced economies, changes in land use regulations explain most of the difference in construction productivity growth between US and other G10 countries.
- Looking beyond these fundamental drivers, measurement issues have also likely played a role. In particular, the US construction output price index has likely failed to fully account for improvements in housing quality, resulting in a downward bias to real construction output and productivity. A recent academic study found that properly accounting for quality improvement and several other measurement problems would raise measured annual US construction productivity growth between 1990 and 2024 from the currently reported -1.0% to -0.3%, still well below the 1.7% economy-wide productivity growth rate over that period. These measurement issues are likely less pronounced for other G10 countries that account for quality improvements using a more thorough bottom-up approach.
- Taken together, our analysis shows that, within the US, the tightening of land use regulations has accounted for 40% of the gap in productivity growth between construction and the rest of the economy since 1965, and the lack of innovation and quality mismeasurement have each accounted for 20%. Looking across countries, we find that more severe tightening of land use regulations and greater quality mismeasurement account for most of the underperformance of the US construction industry since 1991, relative to other major G10 countries.

# Productivity Stagnation in the Construction Industry: An International Perspective

Labor productivity in the US construction industry grew steadily from 1950-1970, narrowly outpacing economy-wide productivity growth. But from 1970 to 2024, productivity in the construction industry fell by 30% while economy-wide productivity more than doubled.[[1]](#footnote__51e0258b-492e-4fb8-9091-dfd94bb6abf7)

#### Exhibit 1: While Economy-wide Productivity More Than Doubled Between 1970 and 2024, Productivity in the Construction Industry Fell by 30% Over That Period

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, Department of Commerce, Department of Labor

The underperformance of the construction industry is not unique to the US. In the last two decades, during which productivity data are more consistently available for other countries, we find that many other advanced economies also experienced stagnation or persistent decline in construction productivity, despite their economy-wide productivity growing at a solid pace. Among all major G10 countries, however, US construction productivity has experienced by far the largest decline and has also underperformed economy-wide productivity the most.

In this week’s *US Analyst*, we explore why the US construction industry’s productivity has underperformed relative to both the rest of the US economy and other countries.

## Driver #1: Lack of Innovation

One partial explanation is that innovation related to construction has been limited. Exhibit 3 shows that most of the major machines used for construction today had already been invented in the 1950s. Since then, technological changes within construction have been incremental.

#### Exhibit 3: Major Machines Used in the Construction Industry Today Had Already Been Invented in the 1940s and 1950s

[[图表：3. Major Machines Used in the Construction Industry Today Had Already Been Invented in the 1940s and 1950s. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research

The rate of adoption of new technology within construction increased sharply in the 1950s and 1960s but has since stagnated. The left panel of Exhibit 4 shows that the share of industrial machines in total construction production costs increased sharply from 4% in 1948 to 12% in 1968, but the share decreased to only 10% in the 1970s and has remained at that level ever since. The right panel of Exhibit 4 shows that pre-fabrication technology—which helps to accelerate construction by producing components of residential units in off-site facilities—was widely used during 1960-1970. At its peak, it contributed about one-third of the total new residential housing units annually. However, its use has persistently declined since then, now accounting for only 5% of total new residential housing units.

#### Exhibit 4: The Adoption of Industrial Machines and Pre-Fabrication Technology Increased Sharply Throughout the 1950s and 1960s but Has Since Stagnated

[[图表：4. The Adoption of Industrial Machines and Pre-Fabrication Technology Increased Sharply Throughout the 1950s and 1960s but Has Since Stagnated. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, Department of Commerce

In addition to a lack of direct innovation, the construction industry also benefited very little from technological advances in other industries. The left panel of Exhibit 5 shows the intensity of innovation in each major industry, measured by patents per thousand workers from 1964 to 2012, and how closely each of these major industries is linked to the construction industry in the supply chain. The chart shows that major technological breakthroughs over the past 50 years have been concentrated in industries like information, communication, and chemical manufacturing that are quite distant from the construction industry’s supply chain. As a result, the economy-wide innovation spillover to the construction industry has remained very limited compared to other industries like pharmaceutical manufacturing and professional management (Exhibit 5, right).

#### Exhibit 5: The Construction Industry Benefited Very Little from the Innovation Boom in Recent Decades Because Technological Breakthroughs Have Been Concentrated in Industries That Are Distant from the Construction Industry’s Supply Chain

[[图表：5. The Construction Industry Benefited Very Little from the Innovation Boom in Recent Decades Because Technological Breakthroughs Have Been Concentrated in Industries That Are Distant from the Construction Industry’s Supply Chain. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, Department of Commerce, US Patent and Trademark Office

Combining patent data with industry-level productivity growth, we estimate that a 1% increase in industry-level innovation intensity can boost annual productivity growth by about 0.2pp (Exhibit 6, left). This estimate is broadly in line with findings from academic studies that also used patent data to assess the impact of innovation on labor productivity (Exhibit 6, right). Our estimate implies that technological changes have only boosted annual construction productivity by 0.8pp since 1965, below the 1-1.3pp boost we estimate for other industries.

#### Exhibit 6: An Industry-Level Panel Analysis of Patent and Productivity Data Suggests That a 1% Increase in Innovation Intensity Can Boost Productivity Growth by About 0.2pp

[[图表：6. An Industry-Level Panel Analysis of Patent and Productivity Data Suggests That a 1% Increase in Innovation Intensity Can Boost Productivity Growth by About 0.2pp. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, Department of Commerce, US Patent and Trademark Office

## Driver #2: Increased Regulatory Constraints

Increased regulation of housing has also been a major drag on construction productivity growth.

In a [prior report](https://publishing.gs.com/content/research/en/reports/2025/10/01/532d47b5-b67c-4428-a2fa-95f1ceacb419.html), we highlighted that land use regulations in the US have become more stringent over time, and that these restrictive regulations are one of the main drivers of the housing shortage in the US. A new international data set constructed by the economists Remi Jebwab, Jason Barr, and Jan Brueckner (2021) reveals that while land use regulations in major advanced economies were generally very accommodative to local population and demand growth in the 1960s, most of these countries tightened their regulations considerably over the subsequent 50 years. Among all major G10 countries, the US implemented the most stringent regulatory changes.

#### Exhibit 7: Land Use Regulations Have Gradually Become More Stringent Over Time Across Most Advanced Economies, with Regulations Tightening the Most in the US

[[图表：7. Land Use Regulations Have Gradually Become More Stringent Over Time Across Most Advanced Economies, with Regulations Tightening the Most in the US. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, Jebwab, Barr, and Brueckner (2021)

Our cross-country analysis further confirms that stricter land use regulations have weighed on construction productivity growth meaningfully (Exhibit 8). On average, we find that a 1% increase in country-level regulation intensity lowers annual construction productivity growth by 0.6pp. But after controlling for other contributors to productivity growth—such as investment intensity, labor quality, and other country-specific characteristics—we find an even larger effect: a 1% increase in country-level regulation intensity lowers construction productivity growth by 0.9pp, holding all else constant.

Our estimate implies that increased regulation in the US since the 1960s has likely reduced annual construction productivity growth by 0.7pp, offsetting much of the estimated 0.8pp boost from technological changes.

#### Exhibit 8: Cross-Country Analysis Confirms That Stricter Land-Use Regulation Weighed on Construction Productivity Growth Meaningfully, Even After Controlling for Effects from Other Channels

[[图表：8. Cross-Country Analysis Confirms That Stricter Land-Use Regulation Weighed on Construction Productivity Growth Meaningfully, Even After Controlling for Effects from Other Channels. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research

As noted in our [prior work](https://publishing.gs.com/content/research/en/reports/2025/10/01/532d47b5-b67c-4428-a2fa-95f1ceacb419.html), land use regulations can take many different forms. Using US state-level data, our analysis further shows that approval delays tend to impose the largest drag on construction productivity growth (Exhibit 9). Rules that restrict the size and height of new buildings also weigh on construction productivity growth, likely because they lead to inefficient investment decisions across different locations. State political involvement, local approval requirements, and impact fees can also lower construction productivity growth, by raising barriers for new developers to enter, thereby reducing local competition.

#### Exhibit 9: Many Types of Regulations Weigh on Construction Productivity Growth, Especially Approval Delays

[[图表：9. Many Types of Regulations Weigh on Construction Productivity Growth, Especially Approval Delays. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, Department of Commerce, Zell/Lurie Real Estate Center

## Driver #3: Mismeasurement in Real Construction Output

Looking beyond these fundamental drivers, measurement issues have also likely played a role. Exhibit 10 shows that the construction output price index has risen at a much faster pace than the aggregate output price index since the 1990s across advanced economies, with the US exhibiting the largest divergence. This raises the concern that the construction output price index might have failed to fully account for improvements in housing quality, resulting in a persistent downward bias to real construction output and productivity.

#### Exhibit 10: The Construction Output Price Index—Which Has Risen at a Much Faster Pace Than the Aggregate Output Price Index in the Last Two Decades—Has Likely Failed to Account for Improvements in Housing Quality, Resulting in a Persistent Downward Bias to Real Construction Output

[[图表：10. The Construction Output Price Index—Which Has Risen at a Much Faster Pace Than the Aggregate Output Price Index in the Last Two Decades—Has Likely Failed to Account for Improvements in Housing Quality, Resulting in a Persistent Downward Bias to Real Construction Output. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, Eurostat, Department of Commerce

In theory, a price index is designed to capture price changes for a specific product over time, while holding the product’s features and quality constant. However, constructing a price index for new residential and non-residential structures is especially challenging because they are highly heterogenous and have many characteristics that determine their overall quality. For example, even with identical features, a slight change in the location of the structure would affect the price. There are two commonly used approaches to construct an output price index for new structures. The first is a top-down output price approach, and the second is a bottom-up input price approach.

The US construction output price index relies heavily on the first approach.[[2]](#footnote__a6c37743-343e-471c-8307-24da23af0b00) For new residential units, the statistical agency uses data on final sales prices of new residential housing units and a set of observed characteristics (summarized in the first column of Exhibit 11). These characteristics are used to ensure that housing units of the “same quality” are consistently tracked over time. However, over the past few decades, there have been meaningful quality improvements in areas such as construction materials, electrical systems, and heating and air conditioning, which are not adequately captured by the current estimation strategy (the second column of Exhibit 11).

#### Exhibit 11: The US Construction Price Index for New Residential Housing Units Likely Missed Some Quality Improvements, Leading to a Downward Bias to Real Construction Output and Productivity

[[图表：11. The US Construction Price Index for New Residential Housing Units Likely Missed Some Quality Improvements, Leading to a Downward Bias to Real Construction Output and Productivity. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research

A recent academic study by Fed economists Daniel Garcia and Raven Molloy (2023) found that failure to account for these quality changes likely resulted in a 0.5pp drag on annual construction productivity growth in the US since 1990. In addition, the official statistics also use the residential housing price index as a deflator for nonresidential structures (Exhibit 12), which likely contributed to an additional 0.2pp drag on annual construction productivity growth. This means that in the absence of these mismeasurement issues, average annual labor productivity growth in the US construction industry from 1990 to 2024 would have been -0.3% instead of -1.0%.

#### Exhibit 12: The Residential Housing Price Index Is Inappropriately Used as a Deflator for Nonresidential Structures, Leading to an Additional Downward Bias to Real Construction Output and Productivity

[[图表：12. The Residential Housing Price Index Is Inappropriately Used as a Deflator for Nonresidential Structures, Leading to an Additional Downward Bias to Real Construction Output and Productivity. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research

Unlike the US, Japan and many countries in Europe rely more on the bottom-up input approach. This approach aggregates prices of inputs used for construction to form a construction cost index. It then overlays a profit margin on top of this cost index to form the final construction output price index. While no comprehensive studies attempt to quantify the mismeasurement bias for these countries, by considering the specific types of material and engineering machinery inputs used, this bottom-up approach likely better captures the nuances of quality improvements over time than the top-down approach used by the US.

#### Exhibit 13: Japan and Many Countries in Europe Rely More on a Bottom-Up Input Approach to Estimate a Construction Output Price Index, Which Likely Better Captures Quality Improvements Over time

[[图表：13. Japan and Many Countries in Europe Rely More on a Bottom-Up Input Approach to Estimate a Construction Output Price Index, Which Likely Better Captures Quality Improvements Over time. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research

Although not all the differences in the cumulative inflation gap between construction and the rest of the economy across countries can be attributed to methodological differences (Exhibit 10), the distinct approaches used by the US and other countries, as outlined in Exhibits 11-13, lead us to suspect that mismeasurement bias accounts for some of this gap.

## Conclusion

Since 1965, US construction labor productivity has been falling at an average pace of 0.6% per year, while economy-wide productivity has been growing at about 1.6% per year.[[3]](#footnote__b631770a-7d50-4402-95d2-71764214d6b9) Our analysis shows that the tightening of land use regulations has accounted for 40% of the gap in productivity growth between construction and the rest of the economy, and the lack of innovation and quality mismeasurement have each accounted for 20%.

#### Exhibit 14: Tightening of Land Use Regulations Has Accounted for 40% of the Gap Between Construction and Economy-Wide Productivity Growth in the US Since 1965, and Lack of Innovation and Quality Mismeasurement Issue Have Each Accounted for 20%

[[图表：14. Tightening of Land Use Regulations Has Accounted for 40% of the Gap Between Construction and Economy-Wide Productivity Growth in the US Since 1965, and Lack of Innovation and Quality Mismeasurement Issue Have Each Accounted for 20%. Data available on request.]](#)

Source: Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research

Looking across major G10 countries for the period of 1991-2024, US construction productivity growth averaged about -1.0% per year, vs. -0.4% for G10 European countries and Japan, and +0.6% for Australia. More severe tightening of land use regulations and greater quality mismeasurement account for most of the underperformance of the US.
